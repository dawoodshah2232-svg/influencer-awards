Creators Just Got Promoted to the Earnings Call

Industry 7 min read By ProFluencer Editorial

There is a sentence no working creator expected to hear this year, and it was said on an earnings call. Between the tariff talk and the inventory cleanup, executives at American Eagle Outfitters told Wall Street analysts how the second half of their marketing spend would lean — and influencer spend made the cut, right alongside digital media and performance marketing. The people who film themselves trying on jeans in their bedrooms are now a line item in an investor briefing.

If you create content for a living, that should change how you price yourself. For a decade, brand money treated creators like a weather event: a budget appeared, a campaign blew through, and then it was gone. What is happening at American Eagle and a growing list of retailers is something else entirely. They are not buying campaigns any more. They are building infrastructure.

The creator community, in-house

Back in February, American Eagle quietly replaced its old creator programme with something new: the AE Creator Community. It is a rewards-based system built around weekly and monthly challenges — styling videos, try-ons, haul content — rather than one-off sponsored posts. By early February, 911 creators had signed up, with more than 200 migrating over from the previous programme, according to reporting on the launch.

The point is not the headcount. It is the design. The old model was transactional: a brand paid a creator for a post, and both sides moved on. The new model is relational: an always-on pool of creators producing a constant stream of video, with the brand owning the platform instead of renting attention through a marketplace. One AEO executive summed up the philosophy in four words that will probably follow this industry for years: content as commerce.

Sister brand Aerie followed in April with its own Realmakers Community, built with brand advocacy platform Duel. And American Eagle is not alone. Macy's has been building its Style Crew affiliate programme toward a thousand members; Sephora launched My Sephora Storefront, a creator-driven affiliate marketplace. Read Marketing Dive's coverage of the retail beat for a month and a pattern emerges: every serious retailer is now running a gamified, always-on creator programme rather than a seasonal campaign calendar.

Why an earnings call matters more than a brand deal

Earnings calls are the most scripted conversations in business. Nothing gets mentioned unless the company believes it reassures investors. When a CFO volunteers that marketing dollars are shifting toward influencer spend, he is not flattering creators — he is telling shareholders that creator spend is now predictable enough, measurable enough, and material enough to discuss in public.

That is a promotion with consequences. Experiment money can vanish in a bad quarter; budget-line money gets defended. When influence sits on the same spreadsheet as paid search and performance marketing, it gets judged the same way too — on conversion, on retention, on proof that the money worked. The creators who survive that scrutiny will be the ones who can show numbers, not just vibes.

"The back half spend is more weighted toward digital media, performance marketing, influencer spend, more day-to-day traffic-driving elements." — AEO CFO Michael Mathias, on the company's earnings call

There is a second consequence, and it arrived on 1 October: a new wave of creator marketplaces where the terms are public. Nexa, which launched this week, lets creators set their own rates, keeps nothing from the creator's side of the deal, and charges the brand a platform fee instead. Love or doubt any single platform, the direction is unmistakable — toward transparent pricing and professionalised terms. The haggle-in-the-DMs era of brand deals is being replaced by something that looks, frankly, like real procurement.

What the promotion actually demands from you

None of this means every creator gets a raise. Institutional money is pickier than experiment money. But it does reward a different set of habits, and most of them are within reach of creators who are already working hard:

Treat your feed like a media channel, not a highlight reel. Always-on programmes want always-on creators. Weekly challenges reward the people who post reliably, not the ones who appear once with a viral hit. The algorithm already favours consistency; now the budgets do too.

Keep receipts. When brands spend like advertisers instead of like patrons, they will ask advertiser questions: what did my money do? Save your analytics, track your affiliate clicks, note which formats convert. The creator who walks into a negotiation with proof beats the one who walks in with follower counts.

Own something verifiable. Here is the part the industry keeps tripping over: the more money that flows through creator programmes, the more valuable fraud and fakery become. Bought followers, engagement pods, rented audiences — the verification problem follows the money. Anything independently verified about you — your votes, your audience, your ranking — becomes a genuine competitive asset when a brand's finance team is reviewing the spreadsheet.

Recognition is becoming infrastructure too

Step back and the arc is clear. First, creators went from hobbyists to vendors. Then from vendors to marketing channels. Now they are becoming something the finance department recognises — a budgeted, measured, recurring input to revenue. At each step, the industry has had to invent the infrastructure the new status required: marketplaces, then advocacy platforms, then rewards communities.

The next missing piece is public, verifiable recognition — a way for the market to see, independently, who the audience actually backs.
